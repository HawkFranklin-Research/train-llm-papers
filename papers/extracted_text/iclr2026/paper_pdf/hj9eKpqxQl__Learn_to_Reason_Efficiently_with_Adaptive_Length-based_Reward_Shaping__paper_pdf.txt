ABSTRACT
Large Reasoning Models (LRMs) have shown remarkable capabilities in solving
complex problems through reinforcement learning (RL), particularly by generating
long reasoning traces. However, these extended outputs often exhibit substantial
redundancy, which limits the efficiency of LRMs. In this paper, we investigate
RL-based approaches to promote reasoning efficiency. Specifically, we first present
a unified framework that formulates various efficient reasoning methods through
the lens of length-based reward shaping. Building on this perspective, we propose a
novel Length-bAsed StEp Reward shaping method (LASER), which employs a step
function as the reward based on target length. LASER surpasses previous methods,
achieving a superior trade-off between performance and efficiency. Next, we
further extend LASER based on two key intuitions: (1) The reasoning behavior of
the model evolves dynamically during training, necessitating reward specifications
that are also adaptive and dynamic; (2) Rather than uniformly encouraging shorter
or longer chains of thought (CoT), we posit that length-based reward shaping should
be difficulty-aware i.e., it should penalize lengthy CoTs more for easy queries.
This approach is expected to facilitate a combination of fast and slow thinking,
leading to a better overall tradeoff. The resulting method is termed LASER-D
(Dynamic and Difficulty-aware). Experiments on five open-weight models from
1.5B to 32B demonstrate that our approach significantly enhances both reasoning
performance and response length efficiency. For instance, LASER-D achieves a
+5.3 improvement on AIME2024 while reducing token usage by 64%. Further
analysis reveals that our RL-based compression produces more concise reasoning
patterns with less redundant “self-reflections”. All resources (Models, Code, Data)
are available at https://github.com/hkust-nlp/Laser.

INTRODUCTION
Recent advancements leveraging reinforcement learning (RL) (DeepSeek-AI et al., 2025; Kimi et al.,
2025; OpenAI et al., 2024; Zeng et al., 2025) demonstrate that LLMs can evolve into powerful Large
Reasoning Models (LRMs), capable of producing extended chains of thought (CoT) to enhance their
reasoning abilities. However, these longer reasoning trajectories come at the cost of increased token
usage and potentially incorporate more compounding errors. Many of the generated tokens tend to be
unnecessarily verbose. For example, LRMs may output thousands of tokens to solve elementary math
problems that could otherwise be addressed within just a few hundred tokens, as shown in Figure 1
(right). This phenomenon is commonly referred to as the over-thinking issue (Chen et al., 2025).
Recently, RL-based CoT compression methods have demonstrated the most favorable balance between
token efficiency and accuracy (Kimi et al., 2025; Arora & Zanette, 2025; Aggarwal & Welleck, 2025;
Hou et al., 2025; Fang et al., 2025; Xu et al., 2025). These methods can be broadly categorized
into three classes as shown in Table 1: budget (Aggarwal & Welleck, 2025; Xu et al., 2025; Qi
et al., 2025), adaptive (Fang et al., 2025; Tu et al., 2025), and full (Kimi et al., 2025; Hao et al.,
2024; Wu et al., 2025). Budget constrains the model to reason within a fixed response or thinking
length. Adaptive leverages a dual-mode design that switches between thinking and non-thinking (or

in practice, they tend to collapse into a degenerate pattern: skipping thinking on simple questions
but still expending redundant reasoning effort on harder ones. By contrast, models trained under the
full mode reason significantly more efficiently even on difficult problems. Yet achieving accuracy
improvements alongside such compression remains challenging. As shown in Table 1, most prior
methods across all three modes struggle to simultaneously deliver substantial token reductions on
hard problems and consistent accuracy gains. Some even rely on multi-stage pipelines with additional
SFT, but still fall short of this goal.
In this paper, we follow the line of full CoT compression, aiming to improve reasoning efficiency
and accuracy through concise, principled single-stage methods. We begin with a simple baseline
in full mode that yields surprisingly effective results (§2). Specifically, we further train long CoT
reasoning models using RL with a rule-based correctness reward, while restricting the context window
size to a smaller value than the model’s typical generated length, so that long responses will be
truncated. This approach can substantially improve token efficiency with only a modest reduction in
accuracy. To better understand this truncation method and to connect it with other RL approaches that
incorporate length-based rewards (Kimi et al., 2025; Arora & Zanette, 2025; Aggarwal & Welleck,
2025), we introduce a unified length-based reward shaping perspective that encompasses various
RL strategies for mitigating overthinking (§3). Building on this reward shaping formulation, we
recognize the limitations of previous length-based reward methods and propose a novel reward
shaping approach (§3.3) that employs a step function guided by a desired target length. We refer to
this approach as LASER (Length-bAsed StEp Reward). LASER achieves the best trade-off between
reasoning performance and efficiency among all evaluated baselines, delivering consistent gains in
both reasoning efficiency and accuracy.
Next, we identify two key points that are lacking in LASER: (1) the desired reasoning length should
evolve during training as the model’s reasoning behaviors dynamically change, and (2) rather than
uniformly encouraging short or long CoT, length-based reward shaping should be difficulty-aware –
allowing harder questions a higher token limit while constraining easier questions to fewer tokens. To
this end, we propose a Dynamic and Difficulty-aware Length-bAsed StEp Reward for RL (LASER-D),
which adaptively applies different length reward shaping based on problem difficulty. Notably, our
algorithm is fully automated with an integrated automatic adapting module, eliminating the need for
manual procedural tuning.

TRUNCATION: A SIMPLE YET EFFECTIVE BASELINE
In this section, we a simple yet effective full-mode baseline, where we simply set max generation
length to a value significantly smaller than the model’s original context window during RL training
(e.g., 8,192 vs. 32,768 tokens in DeepSeek-R1-Distilled). Responses exceeding this limit are
truncated and treated as incorrect, encouraging concise yet accurate reasoning. This baseline has also
been explored recently in concurrent works Hou et al. (2025); Luo et al. (2025). In our experiments,
we adopt DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-1.5B as the base model and investigate the effects of truncation
by limiting maximum generation lengths to 4,096, 6,144, and 8,192 tokens.
Effectiveness of Truncation
Table 2 presents the performance of models across various benchmarks
under different truncation sizes. Compared to the original model, surprisingly, RL training with a
context window of 8192 tokens achieves a substantial 71% improvement in token efficiency, while
maintaining competitive accuracy with a 1.6 absolute point degradation on average. This demonstrates
that truncation is a simple yet effective approach for enhancing reasoning efficiency in LRMs.
Efficacy-Efficiency Trade-off
Although truncation improves efficiency overall, it disproportion-
ately harms harder tasks. On AIME, accuracy drops by 4.1 under an 8192-token limit and by 9.7 at
4096, compared to only 7% on MATH500. This stems from the higher truncation ratio on harder
benchmarks: over 75% of AIME responses exceed 8192 tokens, versus 15% for MATH500 (Fig-
ure 7). These results highlight the efficacy–efficiency trade-off: truncation reduces tokens effectively,

A UNIFIED VIEW ON TRUNCATION AND LENGTH-BASED REWARDS
In this section, we aim to understand the truncation baseline and other single-stage RL-based efficient
reasoning approaches of the full compression mode through a unified perspective. We first connect
them together via length-based reward shaping, and then derive new alternatives with this view.
3.1
THE UNIFIED FORMULATION
Here we first present a unified formulation, and then we show how the truncation baseline and
other works fit into this formulation. Specifically, we define the reward function with two parts: a
correctness term C(y) and a length-based term S(y) controlled by a control variable λ(y):
ˆR(x, y) = C(y) + λ(y) · S(y)
(1)
In most length reward methods, C(y) = R(x, y), representing the original rule-based reward for
correctness. However, in truncation-based approaches, C(y) = 0 as we discuss below. The term S(y)
denotes the length reward, which varies across different methods.
Formulating the Truncation Baseline
As shown in Table 3, truncation is a special case of the
length reward with C(y) = 0, where the target length LT is enforced by the context window.
ThinkPrune (Hou et al., 2025) is another truncation-based approach, which extends vanilla truncation
by introducing an adaptive target length LA to replace fixed target length LT . They iteratively choose
LA and separate their training into three stages. Table 3 also outlines other formulations, we will
introduce them individually in the following sections.
3.2
CONNECTING PREVIOUS EFFORTS TOGETHER
In this part, we build on our unified formulation in Eq. 1 and aim to connect previous approaches,
below we describe several main categories of them. Table 3 formulates different length-based reward
shaping by different designs for C(y), λ(y) and S(y). Parameter α is a hyperparameter coefficient
that controls the magnitude of the length reward S(y). We provide detailed explanations for each
formulation in Appendix H.
Group-based Reward
In group-based reward, the length reward S(y) is designed to encourage
brevity by assigning higher scores to shorter responses within a rollout group, such as Efficient
Reasoning (Arora & Zanette, 2025) and Kimi-k1.5 (Kimi et al., 2025) as formulated in Table 3.
However, this comparison-based approach can lead to reward hacking. Models tend to exploit S(y)
by generating overly concise responses, particularly for simpler questions. We demonstrate this
phenomenon for the Efficient Reasoning baseline in Figure 8a and Figure 8b in Appendix E, where
training accuracy initially decreases while total reward increases. Additionally, Table 4 shows a
more significant drop in MATH500 accuracy compared to other methods, further supporting this
observation.
Budget-based Reward
Although budget-based reward methods are typically categorized under
the budget compression mode rather than full, some concise single-stage approaches (Aggarwal &
Welleck, 2025) can still be unified into our formulation since they are also implemented through
length-based reward shaping. Budget-based rewards use query-specific target lengths (budgets) and
penalize responses that deviate from these instructions. While this mitigates reward hacking seen in
group-based schemes, it can destabilize training. Models require exposure to diverse budgets, but
in large context windows (e.g., 16,384 tokens), these targets become sparsely distributed, causing
reward fluctuations. Figure 8b illustrates this instability. Using L1-Max as a representative method,
we observe that with smaller contexts (4,096 tokens), it achieves stable reward increases compa-
rable to other methods. However, with 16,384-token contexts, rewards fluctuate significantly and
underperform alternative approaches.

3.3
BRIDGING THE GAP: LENGTH-BASED STEP REWARD
As shown in Eq. 1 and visualized in Table 3, a key limitation of the truncation method is that it assigns
the same penalties to overlong responses as it does to incorrect ones, which may over-penalize long
but correct explorations. To address this issue, we extend it as a novel reward shaping approach called
Length-bAsed StEp Reward (LASER), which adopts a step reward function guided by a target length,
rather than performing hard truncation.
Specifically, we design the length reward term S(y) as an indicator function based on a target length
LT . This function assigns a length-based bonus to responses shorter than LT . We also set the context
window significantly larger than the target length LT (e.g., 16,384 vs. 4,096) where truncation rarely
happens. And the length reward term S(y) is only activated when responses are correct, thereby
improving the efficacy-efficiency trade-off. As visualized in Table 3, LASER closely resembles the
vanilla truncation approach; the only difference is that, instead of truncating long responses, it awards
bonus rewards to correct responses that do not exceed the target length. To balance the correctness
reward C(y) and length reward S(y), we follow a typical setting and set α as 0.5. We also find that
LASER is robust across different values of α (see §5.2).
Empirical results are demonstrated in Figure 1 and Table 4, training with the LASER reward achieves
improved efficacy-efficiency trade-off compared to all previous methods. Notably, it is the first
approach to simultaneously deliver significant improvements in both accuracy and token efficiency
on the challenging AIME24 benchmark. These results establish LASER as a promising reward design
for enhancing the balance between efficacy and efficiency.

ADAPTIVE LENGTH-BASED STEP REWARD SHAPING
4.1
DESIGN PRINCIPLES
We highlight two key limitations not addressed in the design of LASER: (1) LASER requires a
fixed target length, whereas the optimal length should be changed dynamically during training;
(2) Additionally, different questions demand reasoning traces of varying lengths, with simple ones
solvable by shorter reasoning and harder ones requiring longer deliberation.
Therefore, we extend LASER to be Dynamic and Difficulty-aware, which we term as LASER-D.
Rather than using a single fixed target length, our approach dynamically adjusts the target length
throughout training and tailors it to questions of varying difficulty. Concretely, LASER-D decouples
the target length hyperparameter across different queries, allowing distinct target lengths to be
assigned to various queries. Moreover, these target length hyperparameters are dynamically adjusted
throughout training.
We separate queries into three buckets of easy, medium, and hard difficulty levels, based on the
correctness rates within the rollout batch – for each question, we have k rollouts and use thresholds
k/3 and 2k/3 to separate them. As such, we have three distinct target length hyperparameters for
these three query groups. Notably, we perform difficulty assessment for the queries during real-
time RL training and use the training rollout batch, thus it only incurs negligible overhead on the
computation. Being dynamic and difficulty-aware, one challenge raised is how to set the dynamic
processes of the decoupled target length hyperparameters. Next, we introduce an automatic adapting
mechanism, to adapt them without any manual intervention.
4.2
AUTOMATIC ADAPTING MECHANISM
LASER-D is driven by an automatic adapting mechanism that periodically evaluates and adjusts the
target length parameters (LA in Table 3) for each difficulty level. Specifically, we first extract a
small monitoring dataset DM (e.g., 500 samples) from training data that mirrors the distribution of
the training data. Every N training steps (e.g., 20), our approach searches and sets the target length
hyperparameters based on this monitoring dataset.
Each query in DM is assigned a difficulty label d ∈{easy, medium, hard} based on the number
of correct rollouts among its K sampled rollouts.1Since queries in different difficulty levels exhibit
distinct length distributions, we compute the target length separately for each difficulty level. To
do so, we introduce the Expected Correct Responses metric, ECRd, which estimates how many
complete, correct responses can be expected for queries of difficulty d under a length limit l. Formally,
for each candidate length l, we compute
ECRd(l) = Pl,d · Cd
(2)
where Pl,d is the empirical coverage ratio, the fraction of all rollouts (computed over all monitoring
queries with difficulty d) whose lengths do not exceed l. The constant Cd is the minimum number of
correct rollouts used to assign difficulty d (e.g., Ceasy=6, Cmedium=3, Chard=1 for K=8 here).
For each difficulty level d, the monitoring module enumerates candidate target lengths from the lower
bound target length LT tokens up to the maximum context window (16,384 tokens) with an interval
of I, computing coverage ratios Pl,d. We select the smallest target length LA satisfying ECRd ≥1
as the adaptive target length for each difficulty, ensuring at least one complete and correct response.
Intuitively, this mechanism sets the target length as the minimal generation length such that at least
one rollout response is expected to be correct. This approach is reasonable because generating
sequences shorter than this length would likely be detrimental, as correct responses are less probable.
Conversely, generating longer sequences may be redundant, since correct responses can already be
obtained with a shorter generation length.
Selecting the Adaptive Target Length.
For each difficulty level d, we enumerate candidate lengths
from the lower bound LT up to the context limit (16,384 tokens) with step size I. We select
the smallest length l satisfying ECRd(l) ≥1 as the adaptive target length LA for that difficulty.
1Practically, K is set to be the same as the rollout size used during training, to maintain consistency with the
training scenario.

Intuitively, this is the minimal length at which at least one correct response is expected; shorter limits
harm correctness, while longer limits are redundant.
Dynamic and Difficulty-Aware Reward
During training, we apply these monitoring-derived
parameters to implement dynamic and difficulty-aware rewards. Each training question’s difficulty
level is determined using the same classification method described earlier. Easier questions receive
smaller target lengths LA, while harder questions receive larger ones. Since monitoring runs every N
steps, the difficulty-dependent target lengths are automatically adapted to the evolving policy model.
Computational Efficiency
This automatic adapting mechanism adds minimal computational
overhead. By using a small monitoring dataset and evaluating only periodically, our method increases
computation by just 3.5% in our experiments.

EXPERIMENTS
5.1
EXPERIMENTAL SETUP
Setup
We experiment with five capable and representative LRMs across four different sizes known
for their overthinking tendencies: DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-1.5B, DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-7B,
DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-32B, OpenReasoning-Nemotron-1.5B, DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Llama-8B.
We adhere to the original prompt from DeepSeek-R1 (DeepSeek-AI et al., 2025), with the full prompt
available in Appendix I.1. We train these models using the DeepScaleR-Preview-Dataset (Luo et al.,
2025), a high-quality mathematics dataset containing 40K competition-level question-answer pairs.
We leverage GRPO (Shao et al., 2024) as our online RL algorithm. We evaluate the models on four
benchmarks of varying difficulty: MATH500 (Hendrycks et al., 2021b), OlympiadBench (He et al.,
2024), AIME 2024, and AMC 2023. We set α = 0.5 for our methods in all experiments to balance
the trade-off between correctness rewards and solution length penalties. LT is a hyper-parameter for
our approaches because the automatic adapting mechanism will enumerate the target length from LT
to the context window size to select the adaptive target lengths LA, as described in §4.2. Parameter
settings for baseline methods are provided in Appendix I.3, and full details of our training procedure
and evaluation methodology can be found in Appendix I.2.
Baselines
According to Table 3, we train models using different types of length rewards design
and compare our LASER, LASER-D to previous works. Considering the high computational cost of
RL training, we select Efficient Reason (Arora & Zanette, 2025) and L1-Max (Aggarwal & Welleck,
2025) as the representatives, since they perform better accuracy compared to other methods inside
same group and are more close to our settings. For ThinkPrune (Hou et al., 2025), we re-evaluate
their models. We also compare with the recent full mode method LAPO (Wu et al., 2025) and list two
adaptive mode methods, Thinkless (Fang et al., 2025) and AutoThink (Tu et al., 2025) for reference.
5.2
EFFICACY-EFFICIENCY TRADE-OFF
Since there is a trade-off between accuracy and response length, one of the best ways to evaluate
different methods is to compare the efficacy-efficiency trade-off. We start with the DeepSeek-R1-
Distill-Qwen-1.5B model, as its small size allows us to run multiple experiments to investigate the
trade-off of different approaches. To fully evaluate the potential of each method, we adjust key
parameters (α for group-based reward, LT for other methods) to explore different tradeoffs along
the accuracy-length trade-off curve. The full details of different hyper-parameters for different
methods can be found in Table 13. As a result, each point in Figure 1/5 corresponds to a fully trained
model under a distinct hyperparameter setting. Benchmark-level results are reported in Table 4, with
additional truncation results in Appendix L.
As shown in Figure 1, LASER-D achieves the best efficacy–efficiency trade-off. On AIME2024 it
reaches 34.2% accuracy with just 5,750 tokens (a 64% reduction), and across benchmarks (Figure 5)
it obtains 60.3% accuracy with 3,520 tokens on average, compared to 10,177 for the original model.

Ablation Studies
We conduct ablation studies focusing on two aspects: the effectiveness of the
proposed difficulty-aware component and the robustness of our methods under different hyperpa-
rameter settings. We assess the necessity of the difficulty-aware mechanism by removing it from
LASER-D.The results in Table 8 show that removing the difficulty-aware component leads to con-
sistent accuracy drops across benchmarks, highlighting that dynamically adapting the target length
to question difficulty is essential for achieving a better accuracy–efficiency trade-off. We examine
the stability of our framework with respect to key hyperparameters, including the length reward
coefficient α (Table 9), bucket thresholds (Table 10), and ECR thresholds (Table 11). Across all these
settings, our methods remain stable and maintain strong performance, confirming that our design
is robust to hyperparameter choices. This also illustrates the robustness of our in-batch difficulty
estimation strategy. Please refer to Appendix F.1 and Appendix F.2 for detailed results.

6.1
CHANGES OF THINKING PATTERNS
To better understand the changes of response length, we analyze the changes of thinking patterns
over RL iterations on AIME2024 with 16 samples per question. We analyze through two approaches,
keywords counts (Yeo et al., 2025) and reasoning behavior ratios (Zeng et al., 2025).
Shifts in “Self-Reflection” Keywords
“Self-reflection” or “Aha-moment” reasoning has
emerged as an intriguing behavior in LRMs (DeepSeek-AI et al., 2025). Following previous
work (Yeo et al., 2025), we track this behavior by monitoring seven representative keywords:
[“recheck”,“rethink”,“try again”,“wait”, “alternatively”,“retry”,“however”]. Figure 3 shows
that average keywords decline notably as response length decreases, suggesting RL reduces spuri-
ous “self-reflection”. Interestingly, later training stages maintain shorter outputs while recovering
moderate levels of “self-reflection”, indicating more efficient use of this behavior.
Changes in Thinking Behaviors
To more comprehensively capture shifts in thinking behavior,
we employ proprietary large language models to conduct behavior-level analysis. We further an-
alyze reasoning behaviors using the cognitive behavior framework of (Gandhi et al., 2025) with
gpt-4.1-mini, focusing on four representative categories: Backtracking, Verification, Subgoal
Setting, and Enumeration. Implementations and the full behavior list are provided in Appendix G.
Figure 4 shows that Backtracking declines sharply (from 30% to just above 10%) as response length
is reduced, consistent with keyword statistics above, while the other behaviors remain stable, with a
slight increase in Subgoal Setting. These results indicate shorter reasoning preserves core reasoning
behaviors while minimizing unnecessary backtracking, rather than collapsing into shallow heuristics.
7
CONCLUSION
In this paper, we propose a unified view for RL-based CoT compression, unifying various reward-
shaping and truncation methods. Building on this view, we introduce new approaches with adaptive,
length-based reward shaping. Extensive experiments demonstrate that our proposed methods achieve
superior efficacy-efficiency trade-off and significant improvements in both accuracy and token
efficiency. Our analysis of reasoning behaviors reveals that our RL-based CoT compression effectively
encourages models to reason more concisely and productively.

H.2
GROUP-BASED REWARDS
In the context of group-based rewards, the length reward S(y) is specifically designed to promote
brevity by assigning higher scores to shorter responses within a rollout group. This mechanism
functions as a comparison-based reward system that inherently favors more concise responses. Most
of them follow the design C(y) = R(x, y) to keep the accuracy performance of models.
Efficient Reasoning
Efficient Reasoning (Arora & Zanette, 2025) follows the principle of group-
based reward by specifically encouraging conciseness within correct responses. The mean and
variance scalars are computed exclusively from the subset of correct responses, ensuring appropriate
statistical distributions. By selectively rewarding conciseness only when answers are correct, this
approach maintains higher accuracy compared to Kimi-k1.5 (Kimi et al., 2025), which encourages

efficacy-efficiency trade-off, we select Efficient Reasoning as the representative group-based reward

BUDGET-BASED REWARD
Budget-based rewards use query-specific target lengths (budgets) and penalize responses that deviate
from these instructions. And the coefficient α controls the trade-off between length reward and
correctness reward. They come in two flavors: exact mode and max mode. We follow same settings
as L1 (Aggarwal & Welleck, 2025) and set α = 0.0003 for exact mode, α = 0.01 for max mode.
Exact Mode
In exact mode, the model must hit the specified target length LT exactly, and any
deviation (even shorter outputs) is penalized. The design can be formulated as:
C(y) = R(x, y)
λ(y) = 1
S(y) = −α · |L(y) −LT |
Max Mode
In max mode, only outputs that exceed LT incur a penalty. The designs follow:
C(y) = 0
λ(y) = I(R)
S(y) = clip(α · (L(y) −LT ) + δ, 0, 1)
